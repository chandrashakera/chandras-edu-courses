Product-of-Sums (POS) Simplification

⏱ 18 min read 📝 Quiz inside

Every K-map grouping so far has minimized a sum of products — an OR of AND terms. But Unit 1 also introduced the other canonical form, the product of sums (an AND of OR terms) — and a K-map can minimize that too, just by grouping the opposite cells.

In simple terms: circle the 0s instead of the 1s and you get the function as a product of sums.

The Flip: Group the Zeros

To find a minimal POS expression, apply exactly the same K-map procedure — Gray-code adjacency, power-of-2 group sizes, as large and as few as possible — but group the 0-cells instead of the 1-cells. Each group produces one sum (OR) term, and the final expression ANDs all those terms together.

The one thing that changes is the read-off convention. For SOP groups, a variable that's 0 across the group becomes complemented; for POS groups, it's the opposite — a variable that's 1 across the group becomes complemented in the sum term:

Variable = 0 in groupVariable = 1 in group
SOP (grouping 1s)write complemented (x′)write uncomplemented (x)
POS (grouping 0s)write uncomplemented (x)write complemented (x′)

This mirrors exactly how a maxterm Mᵢ is built from minterm mᵢ in Unit 1 — POS grouping is just that same complementation rule, applied group by group instead of cell by cell.

Worked Example — Same Function as the K-Map Page

Take F(A,B,C) = Σm(0,1,2,3,5) — the exact function minimized as A′ + B′C on the previous K-map page. This time, group its 0-cells instead: {4, 6, 7}.

Grouping the 0s of F = Σm(0,1,2,3,5): F = (A′ + B′)(A′ + C).

The three 0-cells can't all go in one group (m4 and m7 aren't adjacent), but two overlapping pairs cover them: {m6, m7} and {m4, m6} — m4 and m6 are adjacent because the two ends of a row wrap around.

Pair {m6, m7}: A=1, B=1 (C varies)   → term = (A′ + B′)
Pair {m4, m6}: A=1, C=0 (B varies)   → term = (A′ + C)

F = (A′ + B′)(A′ + C)

Two ways to sanity-check this is right: plug in a couple of input combinations directly, or note that it must produce F=0 at exactly {4,6,7} and F=1 everywhere else — which is precisely what grouping only the 0-cells guarantees by construction.

Test A=0,B=0,C=0 (m0, expect F=1): (1+1)(1+0) = 1 ✓
Test A=1,B=0,C=0 (m4, expect F=0): (0+1)(0+0) = 0 ✓
Expand: (A′ + B′)(A′ + C) = A′ + B′C — the same function as the SOP result ✓
🚀 SOP and POS are two different, equally valid circuit realizations of the exact same function — not two different functions. Which one is "better" for an actual design depends on the specific circuit: sometimes the SOP form uses fewer gates or literals, sometimes the POS form does. Both are worth computing when gate count genuinely matters.

Common Pitfalls

  • Reusing the SOP complementation rule by habit. The single most common POS mistake is writing 0→complemented, 1→uncomplemented (the SOP rule) instead of the correct, reversed POS rule.
  • Forgetting POS terms are OR (sums), and they multiply (AND) together — not the other way around. Each group produces a sum term in parentheses; the overall expression is those parenthesized sums ANDed together.
  • Assuming the POS form will look similar to the SOP form. They're often structurally very different expressions (different numbers of terms, different literal counts) even though they compute the exact same function.

Applications

  • POS-based design is the natural starting point for NOR-NOR circuit implementation — covered later in this unit — the same way SOP naturally leads to NAND-NAND circuits.
  • Choosing SOP vs. POS based on which has fewer 1s or 0s is a standard practical shortcut: a function with far more 1s than 0s (or vice versa) is usually cheaper to minimize from whichever side has fewer cells to group.
  • Digital design verification tools routinely compute both SOP and POS forms of a function as a cross-check — if algebraic manipulation between the two forms doesn't reconcile, it flags a design error.
