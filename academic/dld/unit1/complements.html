Complements of Numbers: 1's & 2's Complement

⏱ 20 min read 📝 Quiz inside 📖 Mano & Ciletti Ch. 1

A subtractor circuit and an adder circuit are two different pieces of hardware — but building both costs more gates than building just one. Complements are the trick that lets a single adder circuit handle subtraction too, by representing negative numbers in a form where "subtract B" becomes "add the complement of B."

Two Kinds of Complement

For a number system with radix r, there are two standard complements, both defined relative to an n-digit number N:

  • The (r−1)'s complement: subtract each digit of N from (r−1).
  • The r's complement: the (r−1)'s complement, plus 1 added to the least significant digit.

In binary (r=2), these become the 1's complement ((r−1)'s complement, since r−1=1) and the 2's complement (r's complement) — the two forms this page focuses on.

1's Complement

The 1's complement of a binary number is found by flipping every bit — 0 becomes 1, 1 becomes 0. This is exactly "subtract each digit from 1," just phrased as bitwise NOT.

N        = 1010 0110₂
1's comp = 0101 1001₂   (every bit flipped)

1's complement has an awkward property: it has two representations of zero — 00000000 (positive zero) and 11111111 (negative zero, the 1's complement of 0). This isn't just a curiosity; it complicates hardware comparison and arithmetic logic, which is exactly what 2's complement fixes.

2's Complement

The 2's complement is the 1's complement plus 1:

2's complement = (1's complement) + 1

N        = 1010 0110₂
1's comp = 0101 1001₂
   + 1   = 0000 0001
---------------------
2's comp = 0101 1010₂

2's complement has exactly one representation of zero (the +1 step causes 11111111's 2's complement to wrap around back to 00000000), which is precisely why it's the representation essentially every real computer uses for signed integers — not 1's complement, and not plain sign-magnitude.

🚀 Shortcut: you can find the 2's complement directly, without computing the 1's complement first — scan the number from the least significant bit, copy every bit unchanged up to and including the first 1, then flip every bit after that. Both methods always agree; the shortcut is just faster by hand.

Subtraction via Complement Addition

To compute A − B using n-bit r's complement arithmetic: add A to the r's complement of B, then apply the end-carry rule:

  • If a carry out of the most significant bit occurs, discard it — the remaining n bits are the correct, positive result.
  • If no carry occurs, the result is negative, and is itself in r's complement form — take its r's complement to read off the magnitude.

Worked Example — 9 − 6 Using 2's Complement (4-bit)

A = 9  = 1001₂
B = 6  = 0110₂
2's complement of B = 1010₂

  1001
+ 1010
------
1 0011   ← carry out of MSB: discard it

Result: 0011₂ = 3.  Check: 9 − 6 = 3 ✓

Worked Example — 6 − 9 Using 2's Complement (4-bit, negative result)

A = 6  = 0110₂
B = 9  = 1001₂
2's complement of B = 0111₂

  0110
+ 0111
------
  1101   ← no carry out of MSB: result is negative

2's complement of 1101₂ = 0011₂ = 3
Result: −3.  Check: 6 − 9 = −3 ✓
⚠️ The end-carry rule is easy to apply backwards. A carry-out means discard it, keep the sum (positive result) — it does not mean "add the carry back in" the way it does in ordinary unsigned addition. Confusing this with the unsigned "end-around carry" rule from 1's complement subtraction is a common source of errors.

Common Pitfalls

  • Using 1's complement's end-around-carry rule for 2's complement subtraction (or vice versa) — the two systems have genuinely different carry-handling rules, and mixing them up gives a wrong result.
  • Forgetting that 1's complement has two zeros — a comparison or "is this zero?" check written for 2's complement hardware will silently misbehave if fed 1's complement data.
  • Applying the complement to only part of the number — complementing must cover every bit of the fixed word width being used, including any leading zeros that are part of that width.

Applications

  • Every modern CPU's integer arithmetic unit represents signed integers in 2's complement — it's essentially universal in real hardware, precisely because one adder circuit handles both addition and subtraction.
  • Overflow detection logic in ALUs is built directly on top of 2's complement's carry/sign behavior — comparing the carry into and out of the sign bit position is the standard way to detect signed overflow.
  • 1's complement still appears in networking — the classic Internet checksum (used in IPv4 and TCP headers) is explicitly defined using 1's complement addition, specifically because it makes the checksum computation end-around symmetric.
