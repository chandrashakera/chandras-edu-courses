Digital Logic Gates Overview

⏱ 18 min read 📝 Quiz inside 📖 Mano & Ciletti Ch. 2

Every theorem and every canonical expression on the previous two pages describes what a circuit should compute. Logic gates are the physical (or, at this level of abstraction, symbolic) components that actually compute it — small circuits that take one or more binary inputs and produce a single binary output, essentially instantly, based purely on the present input combination.

The first three — AND, OR, NOT — are the gates the postulates and theorems on the earlier pages were written in terms of. NAND, NOR, XOR, and XNOR are all derived from those three (NAND = AND then invert, XOR = "differs" built from AND/OR/NOT, as shown in Unit 2's design-procedure example), but they're common enough in real designs and real chips to deserve their own symbols and to be treated as first-class gates in their own right.

NAND and NOR Are Universal Gates

A gate (or set of gates) is functionally complete — or "universal" — if any Boolean function whatsoever can be built using only that gate, in any quantity. Remarkably, NAND alone is universal, and so is NOR alone: every AND, OR, and NOT gate you'll ever need can be built from nothing but NAND gates (or, separately, from nothing but NOR gates).

Worked Example — Building AND, OR, NOT from NAND Only

NOT from NAND — tie both inputs of a NAND gate to the same signal: NAND(A,A) = (A·A)′ = A′.

AND from NAND — a NAND, followed by a NAND-built NOT to invert its output back: AND(A,B) = NOT(NAND(A,B)).

OR from NAND — invert A and B first (each via a tied-input NAND), then NAND the two inverted signals together. This is DeMorgan's law wearing a gate-level disguise: (A′·B′)′ = A+B.

🚀 Beyond the textbook: NAND/NOR universality isn't just a theoretical curiosity — it's a real fabrication decision. NAND and NOR gates are typically built from fewer transistors at the CMOS level than AND or OR gates (which, internally, are usually a NAND/NOR followed by an extra inverter stage anyway). Because of this, real chip fabrication overwhelmingly standardizes on NAND (or NOR) as the base gate, and synthesis tools convert whole designs into NAND-only or NOR-only networks as a standard optimization step.

Common Pitfalls

  • Confusing NAND's truth table with AND's. NAND is 0 only in the all-1s row — everywhere else it's 1 — the exact opposite pattern from AND, which is easy to invert by accident when working quickly.
  • Assuming XOR and XNOR are "just OR and NOR with extra steps." They're genuinely distinct functions (XOR = "exactly one input differs," not "at least one input is 1") — substituting OR for XOR in a design changes its behavior on the all-1s input.
  • Thinking universality means "NAND is the only gate you should ever use." NAND-only implementations are common in fabrication for cost reasons, but for readability, hand-drawn and taught circuit diagrams still typically use whichever gate makes the design clearest.

Applications

  • Every combinational and sequential circuit in this course — adders, multiplexers, flip-flops, counters — is ultimately built from some combination of these seven gates.
  • Standard-cell chip design libraries are built around a small set of primitive cells, almost always including NAND/NOR as the cheapest, most heavily reused gates on the die.
  • XOR specifically is the core building block of every binary adder's sum output (covered in Unit 3) and of parity generation (covered earlier in this unit) — it shows up constantly once arithmetic circuits are involved.
