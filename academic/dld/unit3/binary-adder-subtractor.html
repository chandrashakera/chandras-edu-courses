Binary Adder & Subtractor

⏱ 22 min read 📝 Quiz inside

Addition is the single most common operation in any digital system — even subtraction, multiplication, and comparison typically get built from adder circuits underneath. This topic builds the adder up in three stages: a single-bit half-adder, a single-bit full-adder (which the design procedure from the previous topic will feel very familiar for), and then an n-bit adder made by chaining full-adders together.

In simple terms: a half adder adds two bits; a full adder also adds the incoming carry; chain full adders to add whole numbers, and an XOR per bit lets the same circuit subtract.

Half-Adder

A half-adder takes two single bits A and B and produces their sum S and carry C — but it has no way to accept a carry-in from a previous stage, which is exactly why it's only "half" of what's needed for a multi-bit adder.

ABS (Sum)C (Carry)
0000
0110
1010
1101

The sum column is exactly the XOR truth table, and the carry column is exactly AND: S = A ⊕ B and C = AB — no K-map needed, both are recognizable directly.

Half adder: S = A ⊕ B, C = AB.

Full-Adder

A full-adder adds three bits — A, B, and a carry-in Cin from the previous (less significant) stage — producing a sum S and a carry-out Cout. This is what actually chains into a multi-bit adder.

ABCinSCout
00000
00110
01010
01101
10010
10101
11001
11111

S = A ⊕ B ⊕ Cin (1 whenever an odd number of the three inputs are 1). Cout = AB + BCin + ACin — and that expression should look immediately familiar: it's the exact 3-input majority function K-mapped in the previous topic. That's not a coincidence — Cout is 1 exactly when 2 or more of A, B, Cin are 1, which is the majority condition.

The cleanest way to build a full-adder is to reuse two half-adders plus one OR gate, rather than implementing the SOP expressions directly with fresh gates:

Full adder from two half adders and an OR gate. Adapted from M. Morris Mano and Michael D. Ciletti, Digital Design, Fig. 4.8.

HA₁ adds A and B, giving a partial sum S₁ and carry C₁. HA₂ then adds S₁ and Cin, giving the final sum S and a second carry C₂. Since at most one of C₁, C₂ can ever be 1 at a time (verify: getting a carry from both stages would require A+B≥2 and S₁+Cin≥2, which is impossible since S₁ is a single bit), OR-ing them together correctly produces Cout without ever needing a 3-input gate.

Ripple-Carry Binary Adder

Chain n full-adders together — each stage's Cout feeding directly into the next stage's Cin — and the result adds two n-bit binary numbers. The carry "ripples" from the least-significant bit up to the most-significant, one full-adder delay at a time (the one real drawback: the final, most-significant sum bit isn't valid until the carry has propagated all the way through every stage).

Four-bit ripple-carry adder. Adapted from M. Morris Mano and Michael D. Ciletti, Digital Design, Fig. 4.9.

Worked Example — 4-Bit Addition

Add 0110 (6) and 0011 (3) using the ripple-carry chain, tracing the carry from C0 through C4:

Bit position:    3   2   1   0
A            :   0   1   1   0
B            :   0   0   1   1
carry-in     :   1   1   0   0    (C3 C2 C1 C0)
------------------------------
Sum          :   1   0   0   1
carry-out    :   0   1   1   0    (C4 C3 C2 C1)

Result: S = 1001 (9), C4 = 0 — no overflow. 6 + 3 = 9. ✓

Binary Subtractor

Recall from Unit 1: subtraction A − B can be done entirely with addition, by adding A to the 2's complement of B (invert every bit of B, then add 1). A single adder-subtractor circuit implements this by adding one control line, M (mode: 0 = add, 1 = subtract), and one XOR gate per bit of B:

  • When M = 0: each XOR gate passes Bi through unchanged (X ⊕ 0 = X), and Cin = M = 0. The circuit computes A + B — ordinary addition.
  • When M = 1: each XOR gate inverts Bi (X ⊕ 1 = X′), giving B′ on the adder's B input, and Cin = M = 1 supplies the "+1" that completes the 2's complement. The circuit computes A + B′ + 1 = A + (2's complement of B) = A − B.

One XOR gate per bit of B: M = 0 passes Bᵢ, M = 1 inverts it; M is also C₀.

One such XOR gate sits in front of every bit of B (B0 through B3 for a 4-bit adder), all sharing the same M line, feeding into an ordinary ripple-carry adder whose Cin is also tied to M. Assembled into the full 4-bit circuit — same FA chain as the ripple-carry adder above, with one XOR gate (⊕) added in front of each B input:

Common Pitfalls

  • Confusing "carry" with "borrow." Addition produces a carry-out; the 2's-complement subtractor circuit never needs a separate borrow signal at all — subtraction is handled entirely as addition of a complemented number.
  • Forgetting to tie Cin to M in the adder-subtractor. Inverting B alone only gives the 1's complement; without also injecting the "+1" via Cin=M, the circuit would compute A + B′ instead of A − B, which is off by exactly 1.
  • Ignoring a discarded final carry during subtraction. When A ≥ B, the ripple-carry subtractor produces a final carry-out of 1, which represents the sign and should be discarded (not treated as an overflow) — this mirrors the 2's-complement overflow rules from Unit 1.

Applications

  • Every ALU (Arithmetic Logic Unit) in every processor contains an adder-subtractor circuit essentially identical to the one built here, usually with a faster carry mechanism (carry-lookahead) replacing the simple ripple chain for speed.
  • Program counters and address generation inside a CPU use dedicated small adders (often just "+4" or "+1" incrementers, a simplified adder with one input fixed) built from the same full-adder building block.
  • Any datapath needing both addition and subtraction — not just CPUs, but DSP chips, calculators, and embedded controllers — prefers the combined adder-subtractor over two separate circuits, since it costs only one XOR gate per bit more than a plain adder.
