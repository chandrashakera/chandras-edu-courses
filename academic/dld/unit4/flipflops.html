Flip-Flops & Timing

⏱ 20 min read 📝 Quiz inside

Latch vs. Flip-Flop

The gated D latch from the last topic is level-sensitive: for the entire time EN is high, Q tracks D. A flip-flop is edge-triggered instead — it samples its inputs and updates Q only at the instant the clock transitions (↑ rising or ↓ falling edge), and ignores its inputs the rest of the time. That single change is what makes reliable, predictable multi-stage sequential circuits (counters, registers, state machines) possible — every stage changes at the same defined instant instead of drifting based on how long a level happens to stay high.

In simple terms: a flip-flop is a latch that changes only at the clock edge, so every flip-flop in a system updates at the same instant.

Graphic symbols of the SR, D, JK and T flip-flops; the triangle marks the clock input.

Characteristic Equations & Excitation Tables

A characteristic equation gives the next state Qₙ₊₁ as a function of the flip-flop's inputs and its present state Qₙ. An excitation table flips that around — for a desired Qₙ→Qₙ₊₁ transition, what input values are required to produce it. Excitation tables are the direct link between "what sequence of states do I want" and "how do I wire the inputs," and they're exactly what synchronous counter design (next topic) is built on.

The X (don't-care) entries are the whole point of using JK or T over D in counter design: whenever an input can be either 0 or 1 without affecting the transition, that freedom is exactly what a later K-map minimization step gets to exploit. D has no don't-cares — every transition pins down D exactly. SR has two (0 → 0 and 1 → 1), fewer than JK, and S and R must never both be 1, the forbidden state inherited from the latch.

The Race-Around Problem

A level-triggered JK latch has a subtle flaw: with J=K=1, the characteristic equation says "toggle" — but if the clock pulse stays high longer than the gate propagation delay tpd, the output toggles again, and again, for as long as the pulse lasts. The final state when the clock finally drops depends on exactly how many times it toggled — unpredictable, and effectively random from the designer's point of view. This is the race-around condition.

The Master-Slave Solution

The fix is to chain two latches — a master and a slave — controlled by opposite phases of the clock, so the two halves are never transparent at the same time:

Master-slave flip-flop: the master follows J, K while CLK = 1; the slave copies it while CLK = 0.

While CLK=1: the master is transparent and absorbs J, K to form its internal state Y — but the slave's clock input is the inverted clock, so it's 0 and the slave is latched, isolating Q from whatever the master is doing. While CLK=0: the master locks its state Y; the slave's inverted clock is now 1, so the slave becomes transparent and copies Q=Y through. The output Q only ever changes on the falling edge, and by the time it does, the master is already locked and can't be affected by its own output — no race, no matter how long either clock phase lasts.

Setup Time, Hold Time & Clock Skew

Setup time tsu and hold time th around the active clock edge.

  • Setup time (tsu): the minimum time D must be stable before the active clock edge — in the diagram, the span marked tsu before the dashed clock edge.
  • Hold time (th): the minimum time D must stay stable after the active clock edge.
  • Clock skew: the difference in clock arrival time at different flip-flops in the same circuit, caused by unequal interconnect delay. Skew effectively eats into the available setup/hold margin — enough skew can make a flip-flop sample data before it's actually stable, even if the design looked correct on paper.

Violating setup or hold time doesn't just risk reading the wrong value — it risks metastability, where the flip-flop's output hovers at an invalid voltage between 0 and 1 before eventually resolving unpredictably. Real designs budget setup/hold margin explicitly rather than treating it as an afterthought.

Common Pitfalls

  • Confusing a JK latch with a JK flip-flop. The race-around condition is specifically a level-triggered latch problem; a properly built (e.g., master-slave or genuinely edge-triggered) JK flip-flop does not race-around, because it never stays transparent for a full clock pulse.
  • Reading the excitation table backwards. The characteristic equation answers "given the inputs, what's the next state?" The excitation table answers the reverse question — "given the desired transition, what inputs are needed?" — which is the direction actually needed for counter/state-machine design.
  • Treating don't-cares as "either value works, pick arbitrarily." They do mean either value is logically valid for that one transition, but the whole reason JK/T excitation tables are full of X's is so a later K-map step can choose whichever value simplifies the overall equation — arbitrary per-row choices throw that optimization opportunity away.

Applications

  • D flip-flops are the default choice in modern synchronous design (registers, pipeline stages) because a single input maps directly to the next state with zero don't-cares to manage.
  • JK flip-flops remain the standard teaching example for counter design because of their rich don't-care structure, and historically were common in TTL counter/register ICs (e.g., 74LS76).
  • T flip-flops are the natural building block for ripple/toggle counters (next two topics) — a T flip-flop wired with T permanently tied to 1 toggles every clock, which is exactly the behavior a binary counter's least significant bit needs.
