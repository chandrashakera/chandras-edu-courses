Flip-Flops

⏱ 20 min read 📝 Quiz inside 📖 Mano, Ch. 5

Latch vs. Flip-Flop

The gated D latch from the last topic is level-sensitive: for the entire time EN is high, Q tracks D. A flip-flop is edge-triggered instead — it samples its inputs and updates Q only at the instant the clock transitions (↑ rising or ↓ falling edge), and ignores its inputs the rest of the time. That single change is what makes reliable, predictable multi-stage sequential circuits — counters, registers, an entire CPU datapath — possible: every stage changes at the same defined instant instead of drifting based on how long a level happens to stay high.

The Race-Around Problem

A level-triggered JK latch has a subtle flaw: with J=K=1, the characteristic equation says "toggle" — but if the clock pulse stays high longer than the gate propagation delay, the output toggles again, and again, for as long as the pulse lasts. The final state when the clock finally drops depends on exactly how many times it toggled — unpredictable, and effectively random from the designer's point of view. This is the race-around condition.

Master-Slave JK

The fix is to chain two latches — a master and a slave — controlled by opposite phases of the clock, so the two halves are never transparent at the same time:

While CLK=1: the master is transparent and absorbs J, K to form its internal state Y — but the slave's clock input is the inverted clock, so it's 0 and the slave is latched, isolating Q from whatever the master is doing. While CLK=0: the master locks its state Y; the slave's inverted clock is now 1, so the slave becomes transparent and copies Q=Y through. The output Q only ever changes on the falling edge, and by the time it does, the master is already locked and can't be affected by its own output — no race, no matter how long either clock phase lasts.

Flip-Flop Block Symbols

Every circuit diagram from here on draws flip-flops as a plain labeled box rather than redrawing their internal gates each time. The four standard types differ only in what's on the input side — every type shares the same CLK input (bottom-left triangle) and Q/Q′ outputs:

SR (Set/Reset), D (single data line), JK (no forbidden state), and T (Toggle) — the CLK triangle at bottom-left is present on all four.

Inside a Flip-Flop: Gate-Level Clocked SR Flip-Flop

The block symbol above hides real gates. A basic clocked SR flip-flop is built from two pairs of cross-coupled NAND gates: two "steering" gates that only let S and R through while CLK is high, feeding a cross-coupled NAND latch (the same core idea as the previous topic's SR latch) that actually holds the bit:

While CLK=0, both steering gates output 1 regardless of S/R, so the cross-coupled pair simply holds its last state. While CLK=1, S and R (inverted by the steering NANDs) can set or reset the latch — which is exactly why this simple design still race-arounds if CLK stays high too long, and why the master-slave structure above is needed in practice.

Characteristic & Excitation Equations

A characteristic equation gives the next state Qn+1 as a function of the flip-flop's inputs and its present state Qn. An excitation table flips that around — for a desired Qn→Qn+1 transition, what input values are required to produce it. Excitation tables are the direct link between "what sequence of states do I want" and "how do I wire the inputs," and they're exactly what synchronous counter design (a later topic) is built on.

The X (don't-care) entries in JK and T are exactly what a later K-map minimization step (used heavily when designing counters and control units) gets to exploit — whenever an input can be either 0 or 1 without affecting the transition, that freedom is what lets the simplification choose whichever value produces a smaller circuit. D and SR, by contrast, have no don't-cares: every transition pins down an exact required value.

Setup Time, Hold Time & Clock Skew

  • Setup time (tsu): the minimum time D must be stable before the active clock edge — in the diagram, the window from x=110 to the dashed edge.
  • Hold time (th): the minimum time D must stay stable after the active clock edge.
  • Clock skew: the difference in clock arrival time at different flip-flops in the same circuit, caused by unequal interconnect delay. Skew effectively eats into the available setup/hold margin — enough skew can make a flip-flop sample data before it's actually stable, even if the design looked correct on paper.

Violating setup or hold time doesn't just risk reading the wrong value — it risks metastability, where the flip-flop's output hovers at an invalid voltage between 0 and 1 before eventually resolving unpredictably. Real designs budget setup/hold margin explicitly rather than treating it as an afterthought.

Common Pitfalls

  • Confusing a JK latch with a JK flip-flop. The race-around condition is specifically a level-triggered latch problem; a properly built (master-slave or genuinely edge-triggered) JK flip-flop does not race-around, because it never stays transparent for a full clock pulse.
  • Reading the excitation table backwards. The characteristic equation answers "given the inputs, what's the next state?" The excitation table answers the reverse — "given the desired transition, what inputs are needed?" — which is the direction actually needed for counter and register design.
  • Treating don't-cares as "either value works, pick arbitrarily." They do mean either value is logically valid for that one transition, but the whole reason JK/T excitation tables are full of X's is so a later K-map step can choose whichever value simplifies the overall equation — arbitrary per-row choices throw that optimization opportunity away.

Applications

  • D flip-flops are the default choice in modern synchronous design (registers, pipeline stages) because a single input maps directly to the next state with zero don't-cares to manage.
  • JK flip-flops remain the standard teaching example for counter design because of their rich don't-care structure, and historically were common in TTL counter/register ICs (e.g., 74LS76).
  • T flip-flops are the natural building block for the ripple and synchronous counters covered later in this unit — a T flip-flop wired with T permanently tied to 1 toggles every clock, exactly the behavior a binary counter's least significant bit needs.
