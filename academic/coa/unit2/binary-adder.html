Binary Adder

⏱ 18 min read 📝 Quiz inside

Addition is one of the most basic operations in a computer — even subtraction, multiplication, and comparison are usually built from it. This page builds an adder in three small steps: a single-bit half adder, a single-bit full adder that can be chained, and a complete multi-bit binary adder made by connecting full adders together.

In simple terms: a half adder adds two bits; a full adder also adds the carry from the bit before; chaining full adders adds whole numbers.

Half Adder

A half adder is the simplest possible adding circuit: it takes two single bits, A and B, and adds them — producing a sum bit S and a carry bit C.

ABS (Sum)C (Carry)
0000
0110
1010
1101

S = A ⊕ B    C = A · B

Both equations can be read straight off the truth table — the sum column is exactly XOR, and the carry column is exactly AND. Here's a half adder built from those two gates:

Half adder — one XOR gate makes the sum, one AND gate makes the carry.

The name is deliberate: it only does "half" the job. A half adder has no carry-in input at all, so it can only be used for the lowest bit of a multi-bit addition — the one position that never needs to carry anything in.

Full Adder

A full adder fixes that gap: it adds three bits — A, B, and a carry-in Cᵢₙ from the previous, less significant position — producing a sum S and a carry-out Cₒᵤₜ. This extra Cᵢₙ input is what lets full adders be chained together.

ABCᵢₙSCₒᵤₜ
00000
00110
01010
01101
10010
10101
11001
11111

S = A ⊕ B ⊕ Cᵢₙ    Cₒᵤₜ = AB + BCᵢₙ + ACᵢₙ

The carry-out equation is worth remembering as a pattern rather than by rote: it's 1 whenever at least two of the three inputs are 1 — this is called a "majority" function.

The easiest way to build a full adder is to reuse two half adders plus one OR gate, instead of wiring the equations above from scratch:

A full adder built from two half adders and one OR gate. Adapted from M. Morris Mano and Michael D. Ciletti, Digital Design, Fig. 4.8.

HA₁ adds A and B, giving a partial sum S₁ and a carry C₁. HA₂ then adds S₁ and Cᵢₙ, giving the final sum S and a second carry C₂. C₁ and C₂ can never both be 1 at the same time, so simply OR-ing them together gives the correct Cₒᵤₜ, without needing any 3-input gate.

Binary Adder — Chaining Full Adders

A binary adder adds two full binary numbers, not just single bits. It's built by connecting full adders in a chain: each stage's carry-out feeds directly into the next stage's carry-in. This is called a ripple carry adder — a carry made at the lowest bit can "ripple" all the way up through every stage before the final answer is ready.

Adding two 4-bit numbers takes four full adders, each stage's carry-out wired straight into the next stage's carry-in:

A 4-bit ripple carry adder — four full adders, each carry-out feeding the next carry-in. C₀ is the carry into the very first (rightmost) stage, normally tied to 0; C₄ is the final carry-out.

In general, adding two n-bit numbers needs n full adders. (The lowest bit could use a half adder instead, since it never has a carry-in — but using a full adder there too, with its carry-in simply tied to 0, keeps every stage identical.)

Worked Example — 4-Bit Addition

Add A = 1001₂ (9) and B = 0110₂ (6), working right to left (bit 0 first) so each stage's carry-out becomes the next stage's carry-in:

Bit position43210
Carry into this bit—0000
A—1001
B—0110
Result01111

Reading the result row left to right (bit 4 down to bit 0) gives 01111₂ = 15. Bit 4 is the final carry-out C₄ — here it's 0, meaning the answer fit in 4 bits with nothing left over. Check: 9 + 6 = 15. ✓

No position here has two 1s, so no carry is generated at any stage — try 0111 + 0011 to see carries ripple.

🚀 Beyond the textbook: the ripple carry adder on this page is simple but has a real weakness — the worst-case delay grows with the number of bits, since each stage must wait for the previous stage's carry before it can finish. Modern CPUs use faster designs like the carry-lookahead adder, which computes all the carries in parallel using extra logic, trading more gates for much lower latency. The ripple carry adder here is still the right place to start — it's what a carry-lookahead adder is built to outperform.

Common Pitfalls

  • A half adder cannot be chained directly — it has no carry-in input at all. Multi-bit addition needs full adders for every position except (optionally) the very first.
  • Each stage's carry-out must feed the very next stage's carry-in, in order — skipping or misrouting a carry connection silently produces a wrong sum for every bit position past the error.
  • Forgetting to include the final carry-out as part of the answer. As the worked example shows, the true result is a 5-bit number (carry-out plus the 4 sum bits) — dropping the carry-out silently loses information whenever it's a 1.

Applications

  • Every ALU you'll encounter later in this course builds its arithmetic hardware starting from exactly this full-adder chain.
  • Decimal-digit arithmetic (the next topic in this unit) reuses this exact binary adder as its first stage, adding a correction step on top rather than building addition from scratch.
  • Checksum and parity computations in networking hardware often reuse binary adder circuits, since a checksum is fundamentally a repeated addition operation.
