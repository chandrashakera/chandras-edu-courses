Risks and Responsible Use

⏱ 12 min read 📝 Assignment inside

This is the final sub-module of Module 1 — and arguably the most important one for your professional reputation.

Every sub-module so far has focused on what GenAI can do. This one focuses on what it gets wrong, and why that matters more than people realise. The engineers and analysts who get ahead in Indian IT are not the ones who use AI most — they are the ones who use it most responsibly. Knowing the failure modes makes you the person who catches the mistakes before they reach a client.

How big is the problem?

  • Reported AI incidents keep rising. Stanford's AI Index 2026 (released April 2026) reports that the AI Incident Database recorded 362 AI incidents in 2025, up from 233 in 2024.
  • Even the best models make things up. On Vectara's public hallucination leaderboard, which checks whether a model adds facts that are not in a document it was asked to summarise, no model scores zero. When Vectara moved to a larger, harder test set in late 2025, error rates rose for most models.
  • It has reached Indian courts. Between 2025 and 2026, several Indian courts and tribunals found filings and even orders that cited judgments which did not exist.

These are not edge cases. They are the normal operational reality of AI tools in 2026. Knowing this is not a reason to avoid AI — it is a reason to use it with a clear understanding of where it fails.

Risk 1: Hallucination

Hallucination is the most misunderstood AI failure mode. It is not a bug in the traditional sense — it is a structural consequence of how LLMs work.

Recall from Module 1.1: LLMs predict the next word based on patterns in training data. When asked about something outside their training data, or something they were not trained on reliably, they do not say "I don't know." They predict the most statistically plausible next words — which can result in a fluent, confident, completely false response.

Real-world hallucination cases

  • Fake case law in a US court (2023). In Mata v. Avianca, a federal court in New York sanctioned lawyers who filed a brief citing cases that ChatGPT had invented.
  • A chatbot's wrong answer cost an airline (February 2024). In Moffatt v. Air Canada, a Canadian tribunal held the airline responsible after its website chatbot gave a customer incorrect information about bereavement fares. The airline had to pay damages.
  • Invented judgments in the Delhi High Court (September 2025). In Greenopolis Welfare Association v. Narender Singh, a petition was withdrawn after the other side showed that some judgments it cited did not exist and some quoted passages were fabricated.
  • Supreme Court of India (2 July 2026). The Court set aside tribunal orders that relied on non-existent judgments and called for zero tolerance of unverified AI-generated citations.

The common thread: the output looked professional, nobody checked it, and real people paid the price.

Why hallucination rates vary

Hallucination rates depend on the model, the task and how the test is designed. A model that rarely invents facts when summarising a document you give it can still invent many facts when answering from memory. That is why different benchmarks rank the same models differently.

Small rates still add up. Even at a 1% rate, there is roughly a 40% chance that at least one of 50 responses contains a fabricated element — and you will not know which one unless you verify.

Risk 2: AI Bias

AI bias occurs when a model produces outputs that systematically favour or disadvantage certain groups — based on imbalances, stereotypes, or gaps in its training data.

Example: In 2018, Reuters reported that Amazon had scrapped an experimental AI recruiting tool after finding it rated women candidates lower for technical roles. The tool had learned from ten years of past applications, most of which came from men.

How to spot and reduce bias in your own work:

  • Test the same prompt with different names, genders, regions or languages and compare the outputs.
  • Be extra careful when AI output affects people: hiring, grading, loans, access or performance reviews. A human must make the final decision.
  • Watch for stereotypes in generated examples, images and job descriptions, and edit them out.
  • Report biased output to your team lead rather than quietly working around it.

Risk 3: Data Privacy

This is the risk most Indian IT professionals underestimate — and it has direct legal consequences under India's data protection law.

The Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act, 2023 governs how organisations handle digital personal data, meaning any data about an identifiable person, such as names, phone numbers, email addresses, Aadhaar details or customer records. The DPDP Rules, 2025 were notified in November 2025 with an 18-month phased timeline for compliance. Under the Act, penalties can go up to ₹250 crore for failing to protect personal data with reasonable security safeguards.

Easy test: before you paste anything into an AI tool, ask "Would my client, or my company's data protection team, be comfortable seeing this pasted into an outside service?" If the answer is no, do not paste it.

Do not paste: customer records, employee details, client source code, contracts, passwords or API keys into a consumer AI tool. Instead: use the AI tools your company has approved for client work, remove names and identifiers first, or describe the problem in general terms.

Risk 4: Copyright and Intellectual Property

Here is what is known and what remains unsettled:

AI-generated code — if you use GitHub Copilot or Claude to generate code at work, your employer likely owns that code under your employment agreement. However, a suggestion can occasionally match existing public code that carries its own licence. GitHub says such matches are rare, and Copilot offers a setting that blocks matching suggestions or shows where the matching code comes from. Always check before you reuse it.

AI-generated written content — India's Copyright Act, 1957 is built around human authorship, so content generated entirely by AI has uncertain copyright status in India. In April 2025, DPIIT set up an expert committee on generative AI and copyright. Its first working paper (December 2025) proposed a licensing model for using copyrighted works to train AI. As of October 2026 this remains a proposal, not law, and questions about AI-generated output are still being examined.

The practical rule for freshers — when using AI to generate work product for a client or employer, disclose AI use where your employer or client requires it, check generated code for licence issues, and always follow your company's AI usage policy.

Responsible use — the practical checklist

Run through these steps every time AI output is going to leave your hands:

  1. Check the policy. Is this tool approved for this kind of work at your company or college?
  2. Clean the input. Remove personal data, client names, passwords and confidential code before you prompt.
  3. Verify facts. Check every number, date, name and claim against an original source.
  4. Verify references. Open every link and citation. If you cannot find it, delete it.
  5. Test the code. Run it, read it, and check licences before it goes into a project.
  6. Check for bias. Would the output treat different groups of people fairly?
  7. Disclose. Say that AI was used wherever your employer, client or institution requires it.
  8. Own the result. If it goes out under your name, you are responsible for it — not the AI.

Dos and don'ts for Indian IT freshers

Do:

  • Use AI to speed up first drafts — then edit carefully
  • Verify every fact, citation, and statistic independently
  • Disclose AI use when your employer or institution requires it
  • Use a tool with web search when you need current facts — and open the sources it shows
  • Read your company's AI usage policy on Day 1
  • Use enterprise-approved AI tools for client work
  • Flag AI-generated content that seems biased or inconsistent
  • Keep learning — AI tools change fast and so do the rules

Don't:

  • Paste client data or personal information into consumer AI tools
  • Submit AI output without reviewing it for factual accuracy
  • Cite AI-generated sources without verifying they exist
  • Assume AI-generated code is licence-compatible without checking
  • Use AI output in hiring or access decisions without human review
  • Claim AI-generated work as entirely your own when disclosure is required
  • Rely on AI for medical, legal, or financial advice without expert verification
  • Assume the same rules apply globally — India's laws are evolving fast

Why responsible use is a career advantage, not a burden

Here is the counterintuitive truth: being the person on your team who understands AI risks makes you more valuable — not less.

Companies deploying GenAI at scale need people who can act as responsible AI practitioners — developers, testers and analysts who use AI to move faster and also know when to stop and check. Every case above failed at the same point: nobody verified the output. If you are the one who verifies, you are the one your manager trusts with client work.
