Fine-Tuning vs Prompting

⏱ 12 min read 📝 Assignment inside

You now understand prompting (Modules 1–2), RAG (Module 4.1) and agents (Module 4.2). This sub-module answers the question that comes up in almost every AI project discussion: when is prompting enough, when should you use RAG, and when do you actually need to fine-tune?

This is not a deeply technical sub-module — you do not need to run a training job to follow it. You need to understand the decision logic, the trade-offs and the cost implications. That understanding is what separates a professional who can discuss AI architecture from one who can only use AI tools.

The honest answer most teams miss

The sensible order is: Prompt → RAG → Fine-tune → Distill. Move to the next level only when the previous level genuinely cannot solve the problem. Each step up costs more time, money and maintenance.

The four-level decision ladder

  1. Prompting — better instructions, examples and output formats (Module 2). Cheapest and fastest to change. Always try this first.
  2. RAG — when the model lacks the right knowledge: private, recent or domain-specific documents (Module 4.1).
  3. Fine-tuning — when the model has the knowledge but cannot reliably behave the way you need, even with good prompts: a fixed format, a specific tone, a narrow task done the same way every time.
  4. Distillation — training a smaller, cheaper model to copy a larger model's behaviour on your task, usually to cut cost or speed up responses at high volume.

When fine-tuning is actually the right answer

Fine-tuning is for form, not facts. You use it to shape behaviour, style, structured output and refusal patterns — not to add knowledge that changes often. Here are the four situations where fine-tuning genuinely makes sense:

  • A strict output format — every response must follow the same structure (for example, a fixed JSON schema for a downstream system), and prompting still produces too many errors.
  • A consistent voice or domain style — responses must sound the same way every time, such as a bank's customer-service tone or a specific report style.
  • A narrow, repeated task at high volume — classifying tickets into a company's own categories, or extracting the same fields from the same document type, millions of times.
  • A smaller, cheaper model — when prompts with a large model work but cost too much at scale, fine-tuning or distilling a smaller model can keep quality while cutting cost.

The three fine-tuning methods you will hear about

You do not need to run fine-tuning yourself to be in this conversation. But knowing what LoRA and QLoRA mean — and why they matter — is useful in AI project discussions.

  • Full fine-tuning — all of the model's weights are updated. Most flexible, but needs the most computing power and storage, because you end up with a complete new copy of the model.
  • LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) — the original model is frozen and small extra "adapter" layers are trained instead. Much cheaper, and one base model can serve several adapters for different tasks. Introduced in a 2021 research paper.
  • QLoRA — LoRA on a compressed (quantised) version of the base model, so even large models can be fine-tuned on a single high-end GPU. Introduced in a 2023 research paper.

The decision framework — four questions to ask

  1. Is the problem a knowledge gap? If the model lacks private, recent or domain facts → use RAG.
  2. Is it a behaviour problem? If the model knows enough but answers in the wrong way → improve the prompt first: clearer instructions, examples, an output format.
  3. Would prompting genuinely fail here? Only if well-tested prompts still fail consistently, consider fine-tuning — and only if you have enough good training examples.
  4. Is the cost justified over time? Count the ongoing effort — evaluation, retraining and maintenance — not just the first training run.

The hidden cost of fine-tuning most teams miss

When teams decide to fine-tune, they usually budget for the training run. The bigger cost comes later. A fine-tuned model must be evaluated, versioned, monitored and retrained — and these are recurring costs, not one-time work.

If the provider updates or retires the base model, your fine-tuned version may need to be rebuilt and re-tested. Treat training data, settings and evaluation sets like code: versioned and kept. The real cost is not the training compute — it is the evaluation, data curation and ongoing ownership.

This is why the decision framework above recommends exhausting prompting and RAG before committing to fine-tuning. For most enterprise document and Q&A use cases, a well-built RAG system with carefully written prompts is the better first answer.

The three things to remember

  • The right sequence is Prompt → RAG → Fine-tune — prompting solves many problems on its own, RAG solves most knowledge-gap problems, and fine-tuning is for the remaining cases where the behaviour you need cannot be achieved through prompting. Jumping to fine-tuning early wastes time and money.
  • Fine-tuning is for form, not facts — use it to teach consistent behaviour, style, format and output patterns. Never use it to add factual knowledge that changes over time; that is what RAG is for. Many real systems combine both: RAG for changing knowledge, fine-tuning for stable behaviour.
  • The hidden cost is lifecycle, not training — fine-tuning creates an ongoing maintenance obligation: evaluation, versioning, monitoring and retraining when the base model changes. Count these costs before deciding.
