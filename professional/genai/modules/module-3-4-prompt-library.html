Your Prompt Library

⏱ 12 min read 📝 Assignment inside

This is the final sub-module of Module 3 — and the most directly actionable one. Over the past three sub-modules you built prompts for developers, analysts and managers. This sub-module shows you how to collect, organise and maintain them as a personal prompt library that grows in value over time.

A prompt library is one of the most practical career deliverables of this course. It is what separates someone who uses AI occasionally from someone who uses it systematically.

What is a prompt library and why does it matter?

A prompt library is your own organised collection of tested, reusable prompts for the tasks you do again and again. Instead of rewriting a good prompt from memory each time, you copy it, fill in the details and run it.

The value compounds over time: every prompt you add and refine saves effort on every future use. There are three specific benefits beyond time saving:

  • Consistency — a tested prompt produces reliably good output. You stop getting variable results because you stop writing variable prompts.
  • Institutional knowledge — your library captures what works for your specific context: your role, your tech stack, your kind of clients. Generic prompts from the internet do not have this. Yours do.
  • Career portability — your prompt library moves with you. When you change teams, companies or roles, it speeds up your ramp-up. It is a professional asset in the same way a portfolio is. (Keep company-confidential details out of it.)

The prompt library entry format

Every prompt in your library should follow a consistent entry format. This makes it searchable, maintainable and shareable with your team.

Library entry template
Name: [short, searchable name — e.g. "Client delay email"] Category: [Developer / Analyst / Manager / Career] When to use: [one line describing the situation] Prompt: [the full prompt, with [PLACEHOLDERS] for anything that changes] Works best in: [tool you tested it in] Rating: [High / Medium / Low] Last tested: [month and year] Notes: [what to watch for, follow-up prompt that helps]

The [PLACEHOLDERS] pattern is the most important element. Instead of a prompt that is hard-coded to one project, you write a reusable template:

  • Instead of: "You are a delivery manager writing to Sarah Chen at UK Insurance about the API delay…"
  • Write: "You are a [ROLE] writing to [RECIPIENT] at [COMPANY] about [SITUATION]…"

This turns a one-time prompt into a reusable template for every similar situation.

Starter library — 10 prompts to begin with

Here are 10 ready-to-use prompts across the roles covered in Module 3. Copy them into your library and customise the placeholders. Test each one before you rate it.

1. Feature code (Developer)
You are a senior [LANGUAGE] developer. I am building [FEATURE] using [FRAMEWORK + VERSION]. Write [FUNCTION/ENDPOINT] that [WHAT IT DOES]. Rules: [BUSINESS RULES]. Handle these edge cases: [LIST]. Output the code, then a short list of edge cases you handled.
2. Universal debug (Developer)
I am getting an error in my [LANGUAGE + FRAMEWORK + VERSION] code. Error (full): [PASTE] Code: [PASTE] Expected: [ONE LINE] Actual: [ONE LINE] Already tried: [LIST] Explain the most likely cause, give the fixed code, and tell me how to confirm the fix.
3. Pre-commit review (Developer)
Review this [LANGUAGE] code as a strict senior reviewer. Check in order: bugs, unhandled edge cases, security, performance, readability. For each issue give severity, line, reason and fix. Do not rewrite the whole code. [PASTE CODE]
4. SQL from schema (Analyst)
You are an expert in [DATABASE]. Schema: [TABLES AND COLUMNS]. Business question: [QUESTION]. Output one query with columns [LIST], sorted by [FIELD]. Then explain it in 3 lines and list any assumptions.
5. Insight summary (Analyst)
Here are the key numbers: [TABLE]. Write an executive summary for [AUDIENCE] in two short paragraphs. Every sentence must contain a number. Do not guess reasons for changes; list two questions I should investigate instead.
6. Report "so what" (Analyst)
Finding: [FINDING WITH NUMBERS]. Audience: [ROLE], who has two minutes. Write a one-line headline, a 3-sentence business implication and two next steps. Mark any assumption as [ASSUMPTION].
7. Difficult email (Manager)
Role: I am a [ROLE] at [COMPANY TYPE]. Recipient: [WHO], relationship: [GOOD / NEW / STRAINED]. Situation: [WHAT HAPPENED]. Goal: [WHAT I NEED]. Tone: polite, direct, no blame. Under [N] words, with subject line and one clear request.
8. Meeting notes to actions (Manager)
Turn these notes into: decisions, action items (table: Action, Owner, Due date), open questions, risks. Use only what is in the notes; write "Not assigned" for missing owners or dates. Under 200 words. [PASTE NOTES]
9. Weekly RAG status (Manager)
Write a weekly status report for [PROJECT] from these facts: [FACTS]. Sections: overall status (Red/Amber/Green) with one-line reason; table Area | Status | Comment; top 3 achievements; risks with mitigation and owner; help needed. Use only the facts given.
10. Mock interview (Career)
You are a technical interviewer for [ROLE] at [COMPANY TYPE], hiring freshers. Ask me one question at a time on [TOPICS]. After each answer, score it out of 10 and give one specific improvement. Start when I say "ready".

Where to store your library

Avoid storing prompts in chat history. Chat history is hard to search, not organised, and gets lost. Your library needs to be a document you intentionally maintain. Any of these works:

  • A single document (Google Docs, Word) — simplest; one heading per category, one entry per prompt.
  • A notes or database app (for example Notion or OneNote) — easier to tag, filter and rate entries.
  • Text files in a Git repository — good for developers; prompts are versioned like code.
  • Your team's shared space — only with your company's approval, and never with client-confidential details.

The best tool is the one you will actually open every day. Start simple; move later if you need to.

How to grow and maintain your library

A prompt library that is not maintained quickly becomes a graveyard of prompts you do not trust. Three habits keep it alive:

  1. Add immediately, refine later. When a prompt works well — even once — add it to the library with a note on what it did well. You can refine the wording later. Keep the barrier to adding as low as possible.
  2. Rate every prompt after testing. Use a simple rating: High (use as-is), Medium (use with one follow-up), Low (starting point only). Prompts that keep landing at Low should be rewritten or deleted.
  3. Review and update regularly. AI tools change fast. A prompt that worked best in one tool may work better in another after a model update. Review your library every few months, re-test your most-used prompts, and update the "Last tested" date.

The three things to remember

  • Save what works, in one place — a tested prompt is worth keeping. Chat history is not a library.
  • Templates beat one-offs — use [PLACEHOLDERS] so one prompt works for every similar situation.
  • Maintain it like code — rate, re-test and update prompts as tools change.

Practice (optional)

  • Set up your library in the tool you chose, with the four categories from the checklist.
  • Add the 10 starter prompts plus the prompts you saved in Modules 3.1–3.3, in the entry format above.
  • Test and rate five of them on real tasks, and fill in "Works best in" and "Last tested".
  • Mark the gaps in the role checklist that you will fill next. Your library becomes Section 2 of your Final Project toolkit.
