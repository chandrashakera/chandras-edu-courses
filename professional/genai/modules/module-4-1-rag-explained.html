RAG Explained Simply

⏱ 12 min read 📝 Assignment inside

Welcome to Module 4. In Modules 1–3 you built prompt engineering skills and applied them to real work. Module 4 goes deeper into how enterprise AI systems actually work — the architectures and techniques behind the AI tools companies are building today.

This sub-module covers RAG, one of the most widely used patterns in enterprise AI. You do not need to build a RAG system to follow it. You need to understand what it is, why companies use it, and how to talk about it clearly — which is exactly what AI-related placement interviews test.

Why RAG matters right now

RAG stands for Retrieval-Augmented Generation. The idea is simple: before the model answers, the system first looks up relevant information from a chosen set of documents, then gives that information to the model together with the question. The model answers from what it was given, not only from what it learned during training.

This one idea — letting the AI read your documents at the moment it answers — is why company chatbots can answer questions about internal policies, products and recent updates that no public model was ever trained on.

The problem RAG solves

You already know from Module 1.1 that LLMs have a knowledge cutoff — they only know what was in their training data. This creates a fundamental problem for enterprise use:

Example: An employee asks the company's AI assistant, "How many days of paternity leave do I get?" A general model has never seen this company's HR policy. It will either refuse, give a generic answer from other companies, or — worst of all — invent a confident number. Even if the policy changed last month, the model would not know.

Three problems are hiding in that example:

  • Private knowledge: company policies, contracts and product manuals were never in the training data.
  • Outdated knowledge: anything that changed after the training cutoff is unknown to the model.
  • No evidence: a normal answer gives no source, so the employee cannot check it.

RAG fixes all three by fetching the current policy document and asking the model to answer from it — and to show where the answer came from.

How RAG works — the five steps

  1. Ingest (done in advance). The company's documents — policies, manuals, past tickets — are collected and cleaned.
  2. Chunk and embed (done in advance). Each document is split into small passages ("chunks"). Each chunk is converted into a list of numbers that captures its meaning (an "embedding") and saved in a vector store, a database built for finding similar meanings.
  3. Retrieve. When a question arrives, it is also turned into an embedding, and the system finds the chunks whose meaning is closest to the question.
  4. Augment. The system builds a prompt that contains the question plus the retrieved chunks, with an instruction such as "Answer only from the passages below and cite them."
  5. Generate. The LLM writes the answer from those passages and points to the sources it used.

Notice that steps 1 and 2 happen once (and again whenever documents change). Steps 3–5 happen every time someone asks a question.

RAG vs fine-tuning — what is the difference?

This is one of the most common interview questions in AI-related roles. Both approaches customise an LLM for a specific use, but they work very differently.

RAGFine-tuning
What changesWhat the model reads at answer timeThe model's own internal weights
Best forFacts and documents that changeConsistent style, format or behaviour
Updating knowledgeUpdate the documents; no retrainingRetrain the model
Shows sourcesYes, it can cite the passages it usedNo
Typical costLower to start; ongoing document upkeepHigher; training plus ongoing maintenance

A simple way to remember it: RAG changes what the model reads; fine-tuning changes how the model behaves. You will meet fine-tuning in detail in Module 4.3.

Where RAG is being used in Indian IT right now

Instead of a list of company claims, here are the three patterns you will see most often in client projects and internal tools:

  • Internal policy and HR assistants — employees ask about leave, travel, reimbursement or security rules and get answers with a link to the exact policy section.
  • Support and service-desk knowledge bases — support engineers search past tickets, runbooks and product manuals in plain English instead of reading through hundreds of pages.
  • Document search for business teams — legal, procurement and finance teams find clauses, obligations or figures across large sets of contracts and reports.

In an interview, pick one of these patterns, explain the five steps for it, and say how you would check that the answers are correct. That shows real understanding.

What RAG cannot do — knowing the limits

The most important lesson from real RAG projects is simple: a RAG system is only as good as the documents behind it. Outdated, duplicated or badly written documents produce poor answers, however good the retrieval is. RAG also fails when the right passage is not retrieved, or when the answer needs reasoning across many documents at once — the model can still hallucinate if the retrieved text is missing or unclear.

RAG in 2026 — what has evolved

Basic RAG retrieves once and answers. Three variations are increasingly common, especially for complex questions:

  • Agentic RAG — the system does not just retrieve once; an AI agent decides what to search for, checks what it finds, and may search again with a better query. Answers to complex questions improve, but responses take longer and cost more.
  • GraphRAG — instead of only retrieving text chunks, the system builds and searches a knowledge graph of entities and their relationships. It helps with questions that connect many related facts. Microsoft Research released its open-source GraphRAG library on GitHub in July 2024.
  • Hybrid RAG — combines keyword search with meaning-based (vector) search. It is more robust for enterprise data where exact terms — product codes, regulation numbers, employee IDs — matter as much as meaning.

For a fresher or junior professional, you do not need to build any of these. You need to recognise the terms, understand what problem each solves, and discuss them sensibly when they come up in a project or an interview.

The three things to remember

  • RAG = LLM + your documents, at question time — the system retrieves relevant content from your knowledge base before the model answers. This gives it access to current, domain-specific information without retraining. It is why company AI assistants can answer accurately about internal policies, product details and recent documents.
  • RAG vs fine-tuning: retrieval vs retraining — RAG is usually the first choice when you need accurate, updatable answers from documents. Fine-tuning is the choice when you need the model to behave in a specific, consistent way.
  • Answer quality depends on document quality — a RAG system is only as reliable as the knowledge base it retrieves from. Poorly organised, outdated or inaccurate documents produce poor answers even with the best retrieval. In any RAG project, data quality is the most important non-technical factor.

Your assignment

This assignment turns RAG from a definition into something you can explain with your own example. Each task should take 15–20 minutes.

  • Pick a RAG use case: choose one set of documents from your college, internship or target job — for example, an exam regulations handbook, a product manual or a company leave policy. Write two sentences: who would ask questions, and what kind of questions.
  • Map the five steps: for your use case, write one line for each step — ingest, chunk and embed, retrieve, augment, generate. Note one thing that could go wrong at each step.
  • Try it by hand: paste one or two pages of a real document into a chat assistant and ask, "Answer only from the text above and quote the sentence you used." Then ask one question the text does not answer, and note whether the model admits it.
