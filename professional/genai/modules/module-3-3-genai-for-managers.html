GenAI for Managers

⏱ 12 min read 📝 Assignment inside

Module 3.1 covered developers. Module 3.2 covered analysts. This sub-module is for the third role in every Indian IT team — the project manager, team lead, account manager, or delivery manager whose work is overwhelmingly communication: emails, meeting notes, status reports, client presentations, and stakeholder updates.

If your job involves more writing and communication than coding or data, this sub-module may be the most useful one in the entire course.

The manager's communication burden

Managers are paid for decisions, but much of their week goes into writing about those decisions: emails, updates, minutes and slides. Most of that writing follows predictable patterns, which is exactly what GenAI drafts well.

A widely cited McKinsey Global Institute study (2012) estimated that the average knowledge worker spends about 28% of the workweek managing email, and nearly 20% looking for internal information or tracking down colleagues who can help. The figure is older, but the pattern is familiar to anyone in a delivery role. Add meetings, status reporting and stakeholder updates, and communication takes up a large share of a manager's week. GenAI directly addresses this — not by replacing the judgement in those communications, but by cutting the time spent drafting and formatting them.

Use case 1 — Professional email writing

Generic prompts produce generic emails. Give the model the same context you would have in your head: who you are, who is reading, what happened, what you need, and the tone.

Prompt 3.3A: Context-heavy email
Role: I am a project lead at an IT services company. Recipient: the client's IT manager, whom I have worked with for six months; good relationship. Situation: our user-acceptance testing will finish 5 working days late because the client's test environment was unavailable for 4 days last week. Goal: inform them of the new date, explain the cause without blaming anyone, and ask for confirmation that the environment will stay available. Tone: polite, direct, solution-focused. Constraints: under 150 words, subject line included, end with a clear request.

Before sending, check every date, name and commitment in the draft. An AI-written email that promises the wrong date is still your promise.

Use case 2 — Meeting summaries and action items

Rough notes go in; a clean summary with owners and deadlines comes out. The key is to tell the model not to invent anything that was not in the notes.

Prompt 3.3B: Notes to summary
Below are my rough notes from today's sprint planning meeting. Turn them into a summary with these sections: 1. Decisions made 2. Action items — table with columns: Action, Owner, Due date 3. Open questions 4. Risks raised Rules: - Use only what is in the notes. If an owner or date is missing, write "Not assigned" instead of guessing. - Keep it under 200 words. Notes: [paste notes]

If your meetings are recorded and transcribed, follow your company's rules on recording consent and on which tools may process meeting content.

Use case 3 — Status reports and project updates

Most IT teams report project health with a RAG status: Red (serious problem, needs help), Amber (at risk, being managed) and Green (on track). Give the model your facts and the exact structure, and it produces a consistent report every week.

Prompt 3.3C: Weekly RAG status report
Write a weekly status report for a payment-gateway integration project. Facts this week: - Development: 80% complete, on plan - Testing: 2 days behind because of 3 open defects in refund flow - Budget: within plan - Client sign-off on security review: pending since Monday Structure: 1. Overall status (Red / Amber / Green) with a one-line reason 2. Table: Area | Status | Comment 3. Key achievements this week (max 3 bullets) 4. Risks and issues, with mitigation and owner 5. Help needed from the client Use only the facts given. Keep it to one page.
Your judgement matters most here: the model will choose a colour from the facts you give it, but you decide whether something is really Amber or Red. Under-reporting a Red item to look good is a common and costly mistake — AI does not remove that responsibility.

Use case 4 — Presentations and slide content

Use AI for structure and first-draft wording, then add the numbers and stories only you have. Ask for an outline first; it is much faster to fix an outline than 15 finished slides.

Prompt 3.3D: Quarterly review (QBR) outline
I am preparing a quarterly business review for a retail client. Audience: client's CIO and two directors; 30-minute slot. Content I have: delivery metrics for the quarter, two major releases, one production incident and its fix, and plans for next quarter. Create a 10-slide outline. For each slide give: title, 3 bullet points, and a note on what chart or visual to use. Put the incident slide after the achievements and frame it around what we learned and changed.

The manager's AI workflow

  1. Collect the facts. Dates, numbers, owners and decisions — from your own notes and trackers.
  2. Choose the structure. Email, summary, RAG report or slide outline; reuse your saved prompts.
  3. Generate the draft with full context: role, audience, situation, tone and constraints.
  4. Check every fact and commitment. Dates, names, numbers and promises must be correct.
  5. Add your personal touch. One specific detail or phrase that is genuinely yours.
  6. Send and save. Store prompts that worked in your prompt library for next week.

The three things to remember

  • Context and relationship define quality for manager communications — unlike code or SQL, every output in this sub-module goes to a person who knows you. Include your role, your company, your relationship with the recipient, and the specific situation. Generic prompts produce generic emails that sound AI-written. Specific prompts produce communications that sound like you.
  • Structure the output you want, not just the content — for status reports, meeting summaries, and presentations, specify the exact sections, tables, and format. AI produces consistent, immediately usable output when given a precise structure. Without structure, you spend as much time reformatting as you saved drafting.
  • Add one personal touch to every AI draft before sending — one specific detail, reference, or phrase that is genuinely yours. This single habit transforms AI-assisted communication from obviously AI-written to professionally effective. It takes 2 minutes and is non-negotiable for client-facing work.

Your assignment

Three real communication tasks. Use your actual work context or realistic scenarios from your target role.

  • Professional email: Write a prompt for a real email you need to send — or a realistic scenario you will face in your first job. Use the context-heavy structure: role, recipient, situation, tone, constraints. Note what you had to edit after the first AI draft and why.
  • Meeting summary: Take notes from any real meeting — a college group project meeting, a webinar, or a team discussion — and paste them into the meeting summary prompt structure. Compare the AI output to notes you would have sent manually. What did the model add? What did it miss?
  • Status update: Write a weekly status update for a project you are currently involved in — academic project, internship, or personal project. Use the RAG status structure. Note whether the Amber/Red items are framed accurately — this is where your judgement matters most.
  • Save all three prompts in your AI Learning Journal under "Module 3.3 — Manager Prompts." These feed into your complete prompt library in Module 3.4.

After completing this assignment, you will have a working set of manager communication prompts that cover most of the recurring writing tasks in an Indian IT management role.
