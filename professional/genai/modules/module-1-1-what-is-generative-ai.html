What is Generative AI

⏱ 12 min read 📝 Assignment inside

You have probably used ChatGPT, Copilot or Gemini by now — maybe to draft an email, explain a bug or summarise a document. But do you know what is actually happening when you type a message and the AI responds?

This sub-module explains what Generative AI is, how Large Language Models work, and why this matters for your career in Indian IT — all without a single equation.

What does "Generative AI" actually mean?

Let us start with the two words separately.

AI (Artificial Intelligence) is a broad term for software that can perform tasks that normally require human thinking — recognising images, translating languages, making decisions.

Generative means it creates new content. Earlier AI systems were mostly discriminative — they classified or predicted things. A spam filter decides: is this email spam or not spam? A recommendation engine decides: which movie should I suggest?

Generative AI is different. It produces something new — a paragraph of text, a line of code, an image, a piece of music — that did not exist before.

The most powerful form of Generative AI for text today is built on Large Language Models (LLMs).

What is a Large Language Model?

An LLM is a type of AI trained on an enormous amount of text — books, websites, research papers, code repositories, forum posts and more. GPT-3, for example, was trained on about 300 billion tokens of text (a token is a word or part of a word).

During training, the model learns one thing at a deep level:

Predict the next token. Given some text, what word (or part of a word) is most likely to come next?

That sounds simple. But when you do this billions of times, across hundreds of billions of examples, the model ends up learning the patterns of human language and much of the knowledge written in it.

It learns that after "The capital of India is", the words "New Delhi" are overwhelmingly likely. It learns that after def calculate_tax(income):, a line of Python code is expected, not a recipe. It learns the tone of a formal email versus a WhatsApp message.

None of this was explicitly programmed. It emerged from pattern learning at massive scale.

The "Large" in Large Language Model

GPT-3, released by OpenAI in 2020, has 175 billion parameters. Each parameter is a numerical value tuned during training to capture patterns in language. Think of each parameter as a tiny dial. Training adjusts all the dials together, over and over, until the model becomes very good at predicting what comes next.

Why does size matter? As models grew larger and were trained on more data, they became noticeably better at tasks such as reasoning through multi-step problems, writing working code and explaining complex concepts.

The industry has also learned that efficiency matters as much as size. Newer models are not necessarily bigger — they are better trained and smarter about how they use their parameters. The exact designs of today's ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini models are mostly not publicly disclosed.

How does a conversation with ChatGPT actually work?

When you type a message, the model does not "think" the way you do. It runs through the same mechanical steps — hundreds of times — to build your response one token at a time.

  1. Tokenisation. Your text is broken into tokens — whole words or parts of words. A common word may be one token; an unusual name may be split into several.
  2. Context window. The model reads all the tokens in the conversation as one long sequence. How much it can read at once — its context window — depends on the model, from tens of thousands of tokens to over a million.
  3. Probability calculation. For every token in its vocabulary — tens of thousands of possibilities or more — the model calculates how likely it is to come next.
  4. Sampling. Rather than always picking the single most likely token (which would sound robotic and repetitive), the model picks from the most likely options with some controlled randomness. This is why the same question asked twice can give slightly different answers.
  5. Repeat. Steps 3 and 4 repeat for every token in the response until the model decides to stop. A 200-word reply involves hundreds of these cycles.

This is why responses stream word by word — the model is generating your reply one token at a time, in real time.

What Generative AI is NOT

Three misconceptions cause the most problems — either leading to over-trust or unnecessary dismissal of AI tools.

It does not automatically know today's facts. An LLM answers from what it learned during training. Some apps add a web search step, but the model itself has no live knowledge, and even with search it can misread or misquote sources. This is why it can hallucinate — confidently state something false. Always verify important factual claims.

It does not understand in the human sense. The model does sophisticated pattern matching. Its output often looks like genuine understanding, but this explains why AI can fail in surprising ways on new or unusual problems.

It does not remember you by itself. The model only sees what is in the current conversation's context window. Some apps add a "memory" feature that saves notes about you and adds them to future conversations — but that is the app storing information, not the model learning from you. Never assume it knows something you have not told it in this conversation, and be careful what personal information you share.

The three things to remember

  • Generative AI creates; earlier AI classified — LLMs generate new text by predicting the next token, learned from huge amounts of written text.
  • Prediction, not lookup — the model produces the most likely-sounding answer, not a checked fact. That is why fluent answers can still be wrong.
  • Know the limits — no automatic live knowledge, no human-style understanding, and no memory beyond what the app gives it. Verify facts and protect personal data.

Practice (optional)

  • See sampling in action: ask any chat assistant the same question twice in two new chats — for example, "Explain cloud computing in two sentences." Note how the answers differ.
  • Test the knowledge limit: ask a question about something very recent, with web search turned off if your tool allows it. Note whether the model admits it does not know or confidently guesses.
